It preserves the legacy platform's pricing logic to the cent while fixing its four known production bugs, and every screen below is running on the new stack right now.

1.Sign in & the product shell

What it is: secure email/password sign-in with self-serve registration, forgotten-password recovery, and a role-aware landing: owners land on their PMS, YoHo staff land on the staff console.

Guided steps

  1. Open http://localhost:3000. You land on the sign-in screen (note the clean dark theme — this is the product default).
  2. Point out the links: Register (self-serve hotel signup — you'll return to this in the staff section) and Forgot password (emails a reset link valid for 60 minutes).
  3. Sign in as owner@demo.yohobed.test / password123.
  4. You arrive on the Dashboard. Walk the left sidebar top-to-bottom — this is the whole product: Dashboard, Calendar, Bookings, Inbox, Customers, Deals, Finance, Reviews, Comms, Setup, Profile.
  5. Click the sun/moon toggle in the sidebar footer to flip to light mode and back — every screen supports both.
The logic underneath
Passwords are bcrypt-hashed; a successful login issues a signed JWT that carries the user's memberships (which hotels they belong to, and their role). Every subsequent request is checked twice: the JWT proves who you are, and a tenant guard proves the hotel you're acting on is yours. Login errors are deliberately identical for "unknown email" and "wrong password" so attackers can't discover which emails exist. A suspended hotel is blocked at sign-in and on every request — suspension takes effect immediately, not at next login.
Wow moment
"One login, two products": after the owner tour, sign in as staff@yohobed.test in a second window — same login screen, completely different application (the staff console). The system routes by role.

2.Dashboard — the front desk

What it is: the receptionist's home screen. One glance answers: who arrives today, who leaves, who's in the building, what needs my approval, how full are we, and how much money is this month bringing in.

Guided steps

  1. Read the stat tiles across the top: Arrivals 1 · Departures 1 · In-house 2 · Occupancy % · Month gross. All computed live for today (23 Jul).
  2. The Pending banner shows 2 bookings awaiting approval — you'll approve one in the Bookings section.
  3. In Arrivals today: Kasun Jayawardena (ref 2607230005, 3 nights, Deluxe Room). Tap Check in — one tap, status flips to Checked-in, and In-house becomes 3.
  4. In Departures today: Sofia Marchetti (ref 2607230004). Tap Check out — status flips to Checked-out… and the system has just emailed Sofia a review invitation automatically (you'll show the proof in Comms, §8).
  5. Scroll the Recent bookings list — the newest activity, each with its status pill.
  6. Use the property filter to flip between Cinnamon Lakeside and Ceylon Tax Villa — every number recomputes per property.
The logic underneath
  • Occupancy % = confirmed room-nights ÷ physical rooms for the day. Only "real" statuses count (Approved / Checked-in / Checked-out) — pending and cancelled bookings never inflate the number.
  • Month gross is summed from each booking's per-night price snapshot, so a stay that straddles two months lands in the right month, night by night.
  • Check-in / check-out are guarded state transitions (only an Approved booking can check in; only a Checked-in one can check out) with timestamps stamped for the audit trail. Check-out is also the trigger that mints the guest-review invitation — no staff action needed.
Wow moment
The whole morning routine of a receptionist — arrivals, departures, approvals — is a series of single taps from one screen. In the legacy system there was no dashboard at all.

3.Calendar — rates, availability & restrictions

What it is: the revenue-manager's cockpit. A full-month wall calendar where every cell shows the selling price the guest will actually pay, the rooms left to sell, and open/closed status — with inline editing and bulk tools.

Guided steps

  1. Open Calendar. Select Cinnamon Lakeside → Deluxe Room. July is on screen; every priced day shows ~Rs 24,390.25 weekdays and ~Rs 33,875.35 weekends.
  2. Click any future cell and edit inline — change the base price and watch the selling price recompute instantly from the pricing engine (not a manual field!).
  3. Step to August. Point out 15–20 Aug: cells carry a −15% drop overlay — that's the "August Getaway" promotion you'll show in Deals (§7), already painted onto the calendar.
  4. Point at 10 Aug: only 1 room left — your segue to the overbooking story ("the old system could sell this room twice; this one cannot" — §14).
  5. Use the bulk toolbar: pick a From/To range (or "Whole month") and set price, open/close days, apply a last-minute drop, or set min/max stay in one action. (14–16 Aug already carries a min-stay of 2 nights — try to book a 1-night stay there later and the system politely refuses.)
  6. Expand Change history at the bottom: every price, availability, drop and restriction change — what changed, for which dates, and who did it.
  7. Switch to Ceylon Tax Villa → Ocean Suite: weekday cells read Rs 31,625 — same Rs 18,000 base, but this property has slab commission and taxes baked into its selling price. 1–3 Aug additionally shows a 15% last-minute drop.
The logic underneath — the pricing engine

The owner only ever types the base price (what the hotel wants to keep). The system derives what the guest pays — in this exact order, matching the legacy platform to the cent:

1 YoHo commission percentage: base ÷ (1 − pct/100) − base e.g. 18,000 ÷ 0.9 − 18,000 = 2,000 or slab: fixed fee by base-price band e.g. Rs 0–20,000 → 2,500 2 OTA gross-up (base + commission) ÷ (1 − 18/100) 20,000 ÷ 0.82 = 24,390.25 ← Deluxe selling 3 Taxes (if any) × (1+SC%) × (1+NBT%) × (1+VAT%) in priority order 25,000 × 1.10 × 1.15 = 31,625 ← Ocean Suite selling 4 Last-minute drop charged = selling × (1 − drop%) 31,625 × 0.85 = 26,881.25
  • All rounding is round-up to 2 decimals (ceiling) — the legacy behaviour, reproduced deliberately so migrated hotels see identical prices.
  • Ceylon Tax Villa's weekday cell proves the whole chain: 18,000 base + 2,500 yoho + 4,500 ota + 6,625 taxes = 31,625. Nothing is invented; the split reconciles by construction — the same split Finance uses for settlement (§6).
  • Every calendar edit also (a) writes an ARI history row with the actor's email, and (b) queues a channel-manager sync event in the same database transaction — so OTAs are told about every change, guaranteed (§14, bug #3).

4.Bookings — the reservation book

What it is: every reservation in one table — walk-ins created here and OTA bookings imported automatically — with the full lifecycle: approve, reject, cancel, no-show, check-in, check-out, and amend.

Guided steps

  1. Open Bookings. Sweep the status pills — the book already contains every state: Pending Dinesh Fernando & Priya Sharma · Approved Chen Wei, Aisha Rahman, Nadeesha Silva… · Checked-in Emma Whitfield · Checked-out Ruwan Perera · Cancelled Liam O'Connor · No-show Harsha Wickramasinghe.
  2. Approve live: find Dinesh Fernando (ref 2607230009, 3–6 Aug) and tap Approve — Pending → Approved. (Leave Priya Sharma pending — the staff console will approve that one, §10.)
  3. Open a booking — use Aisha Rahman (ref 2607230011): a proper detail card with the guest, the stay, the per-night price breakdown (7 Aug Rs 24,390.25 · 8–9 Aug Rs 33,875.35 each = Rs 92,140.95) and her SUMMER10 coupon discount of Rs 9,214.09 recorded on the booking — plus the lifecycle trail (created → approved, timestamped).
  4. Create a walk-in: tap ＋ Book a stay. A full modal: guest name/email/phone, dates, number of rooms, live nights×rooms summary, and a coupon field. Book any August stay — it prices itself from the calendar and lands as Pending, and the guest gets a confirmation email instantly (§8 shows the email log).
  5. Amend: open any Pending/Approved booking → Edit. Change the dates — the system re-prices the new nights and swaps the inventory atomically; if the new dates don't fit, it refuses with a clear conflict message rather than overbooking.
  6. Show Cancel on a pending booking: the room instantly returns to the calendar's sellable count.
The logic underneath
  • One transaction does everything. Creating a booking: verifies the room/occupancy pairing, checks min/max-stay rules for the arrival date, snapshots tonight's prices night-by-night, atomically reserves inventory (impossible to oversell — the database refuses if any night lacks rooms), applies coupon/referral, generates the reference from an atomic daily counter (format yymmdd#### — today's are 260723xxxx), queues the OTA sync event, notifies the owner, and emails the guest. If any step fails, everything rolls back.
  • Prices are snapshotted, not referenced. The per-night breakdown you showed is frozen at booking time — repricing the calendar tomorrow never rewrites yesterday's contracts. Finance settles from these snapshots.
  • The status machine is strict. Cancel/reject release inventory; no-show keeps it consumed (the room was held all night — that's revenue, matching legacy policy); only Approved can check in. Every transition is recorded in the trail.

5.Inbox — OTA reservations

What it is: where bookings made on booking.com, Agoda, Expedia etc. arrive via the channel manager. The core promise: an OTA reservation can never be silently lost — every push is recorded first, then imported; failures stay visible with a Retry button.

Guided steps

  1. Open Inbox. Two clean imports: Hannah Lee (booking.com, 12–14 Aug, Rs 48,780.50, ref 2607230014) and Marco Rossi (agoda, 20–22 Aug, Rs 63,250, ref 2607230015). Both were imported as Approved bookings automatically — flip to Bookings and they're in the book, inventory already decremented.
  2. Point at the failed row: Yuki Tanaka (expedia, 2–4 Sep). The error is displayed: those September nights have no prices yet. The reservation was not lost — it's sitting here waiting.
  3. Live fix (the money moment): go to Calendar → Deluxe Room → September → set a price for 1–7 Sep and open the days. Return to Inbox → tap Retry → the reservation imports and appears in Bookings. A failed OTA booking, recovered in 20 seconds by the owner, no support ticket.
  4. Show the room-code mappings panel: CM-DLX-001 → Deluxe Room, CM-OCN-101 → Ocean Suite — how the channel manager's room codes route to real rooms.
The logic underneath
  • The channel manager calls a secured webhook (shared-secret header; wrong secret → rejected). The room code alone identifies the hotel — and the payload is recorded before import is attempted, so a crash mid-import still leaves the reservation on file.
  • Imports are idempotent per channel + external reference — if booking.com retries the same push five times, you get one booking, not five.
  • OTA bookings import as auto-approved (the guest already paid the OTA — there is nothing for the owner to approve) and consume inventory through the exact same atomic path as walk-ins. An OTA cancellation releases the rooms automatically.
  • An unmapped room code answers with a specific "retry later" signal to the channel manager instead of swallowing the booking.

6.Finance — revenue, invoices & settlement

What it is: the money screen. Monthly revenue by status, guest invoices and payments, and the payout statement — the settlement document that splits every rupee of gross revenue between the hotel, YoHo, the OTA channel, and the taxman, and proves the split adds back up.

Guided steps

  1. Open Finance. The month picker defaults to July 2026. Revenue summary: confirmed gross ~Rs 606,242 across the tenant, with a by-status breakdown (point out that Cancelled contributes zero).
  2. Select property Cinnamon Lakeside and show the Payout statement for July:
    Gross selling = Hotel base (net payable) + YoHo commission + OTA commission + Taxes
    Rs 345,528.55 Rs 255,000.00 Rs 28,333.34 Rs 62,195.21 Rs 0.00
    …and the "✓ reconciles" line: the four parts sum to the gross to the cent. The hotel's payout is the base — exactly what the owner typed on the calendar. Flip to Ceylon Tax Villa to show a statement where the taxes column is non-zero.
  3. Invoices: open INV-2607230001 (Ruwan Perera, 3 nights) — line items from the per-night snapshot, status paid, with its card payment (VISA-4212) recorded in full. Then INV-2607230007 (Tharindu Fernando) — a 50% advance received by bank transfer, invoice still open. Record the balance live if you like: the invoice flips to paid automatically the moment received payments cover it.
  4. Show the saved July payout snapshot — statements can be frozen as payout records for the payment run.
  5. Point to Profile → bank details (§9): Commercial Bank of Ceylon, Kollupitiya — the settlement knows where to send the money.
The logic underneath
  • The settlement split is not an approximation — it reuses the same decomposition the pricing engine used when each night was priced (§3): base + yoho + ota + taxes = gross, per night, snapshotted on the booking. Because settlement reads those snapshots, the statement reconciles by construction — there is no "rounding difference" bucket.
  • Revenue counts Approved, Checked-in and Checked-out bookings (checked-in/out are earned revenue); Pending, Cancelled and Rejected never touch the money.
  • Invoices are numbered INV-<booking reference> and are idempotent — asking twice returns the same invoice, never a duplicate.
Wow moment
"Every rupee is accounted for": gross → hotel + YoHo + OTA + tax, reconciling to the cent, live, per property and period. This is the trust argument against the legacy system's spreadsheet reconciliation.

7.Deals — promotions, coupons & referral partners

What it is: the commercial toolkit: date-window promotions that discount the calendar, coupon codes guests redeem at booking, and referral partners who earn a commission on the bookings they bring.

Guided steps

  1. Open Deals. Show the promotion "August Getaway" −15% (15–20 Aug, Cinnamon Lakeside) — then remind the client you already saw it as the drop overlay on the August calendar (§3). Creating a promotion and tapping Apply paints the discount across every rate of the property for the window, and notifies the OTA channels.
  2. Show the coupons: SUMMER10 (10%, valid 1 Jul–31 Aug, unlimited) and FAMILY5000 (Rs 5,000 fixed, capped at 20 uses, Cinnamon Lakeside only). Tie it back to Aisha Rahman's booking (§4): SUMMER10 recorded a Rs 9,214.09 discount on her Rs 92,140.95 stay.
  3. Show referral partner Island Travels (Pvt) Ltd, code ISLAND, 5% — and the commission ledger: Chen Wei's 3-night Ocean Suite booking (ref 2607230008) earned Island Travels a pending commission automatically.
The logic underneath
  • Promotions are implemented as last-minute drops on the rate calendar — one mechanism, no special cases: the charged price is always selling × (1 − drop%), and removing a promotion later doesn't retroactively rewrite prices already painted (you re-price to undo).
  • Coupons validate at booking time: active, inside its date window, under its usage cap, and compatible with the property. The discount is stored on the booking itself, so amendments keep the original deal.
  • Referral commissions compute on the commissionable amount (not on taxes) and accrue per booking as pending → paid — an auditable ledger per partner.

11.Under the hood — the engineering story (Q&A ammunition)

Four legacy production bugs — found, fixed, and proven with tests

Legacy bug What went wrong The 2.0 fix
#1 Overbooking race Two simultaneous bookings for the last room could both succeed (read-then-write with no lock). Atomic conditional decrement in the database — proven with a test firing 12 parallel bookings at 1 room: exactly one wins, count never goes negative.
#2 Wrong price key Walk-ins priced by the wrong identifier (room id used as the rate key) — wrong amounts billed. Bookings carry the explicit occupancy (pricing) key, verified to belong to the room.
#3 Silent channel-manager push Price/availability pushes to OTAs were fire-and-forget: a failure was silent → stale OTA prices, overbookings. Transactional outbox: the sync event commits with the change, a worker delivers with 5 retries + exponential backoff; final failures alert and stay visible (Inbox/health screens).
#4 Duplicate booking references References generated by "max + 1" — concurrent bookings could collide. Atomic per-day counter with a uniqueness guarantee (20-parallel-bookings test: all unique).

Architecture in five bullets

  • Stack: TypeScript end-to-end — NestJS API + Next.js web + background worker (BullMQ/Redis) + PostgreSQL 16, in a Turborepo monorepo. The pricing engine is a framework-free package shared by API, seed and tests.
  • Multi-tenant security is enforced by the database, not by remembering to add WHERE clauses: PostgreSQL Row-Level Security with a default-deny policy. The app connects as a restricted role; a query without a tenant context returns nothing. Cross-tenant reads 404 by construction.
  • Parity by contract: the legacy pricing formulas (round-up rounding, commission gross-up, ÷0.82 OTA factor, nested tax decomposition) are reproduced exactly and pinned by unit tests with the legacy's own numbers.
  • 105 automated tests: 42 domain (pricing parity), 8 database-integration (RLS isolation, concurrency races), 36 API end-to-end over real HTTP + real Postgres, 19 channel-manager contract tests — plus CI on every push.
  • Channel manager ready: the AxisRooms adapter is code-complete against the legacy integration contract (same 5 endpoints, same wire format), running behind the outbox. It needs only production credentials to go live; the demo uses a stub so every sync is visible locally.

Honest answers for "what's not in yet"

  • Data migration (ETL) from the legacy MySQL — designed (with a parity harness as the cutover gate), not yet run; today's system runs on fresh data.
  • Hosting/deploy — runs locally/CI today; production infrastructure is the next step after sign-off.
  • AxisRooms live connection — adapter finished, waiting on credentials and a test property.
  • Planned next (post-MVP): the AI Supplier Copilot — a chat assistant with read-only, tenant-scoped tools over these exact services ("How was my August? Anything I should reprice?"). The service layer was built MCP-ready for it from day one.

17.Scalability, the future of the YB suite & the budget reality

Scalability — with real numbers

  • The data is small by database standards. 1,000 properties × 30 rooms × 365 priced days ≈ 11 million calendar rows a year — routine for PostgreSQL with the indexes already in place. 100,000 bookings a year is trivial. The demo's whole dataset is a rounding error of what one Postgres server handles.
  • Multi-tenancy scales in one database. Row-Level Security fences thousands of hotels in a single Postgres — the same model used by major SaaS platforms — with sharding by tenant available as a far-future option, not a prerequisite.
  • Spikes are absorbed by the queue. A price change across 60 days × 500 properties doesn't hammer the OTAs — it lands in the outbox and the worker drains it with retries. Add worker processes to drain faster; the API never blocks on the channel manager.
  • Stateless web/API tier = horizontal scale by adding instances behind a load balancer; sessions are JWTs, uploads are in object storage, nothing lives on the server.
  • Proof culture: the concurrency-sensitive paths (overbooking, booking references) are guarded by automated race-condition tests, so scale doesn't reintroduce the legacy bugs.

The future of the YB suite

The market context: channel managers and PMSes (SiteMinder, STAAH, Cloudbeds and peers) typically charge hotels on the order of US$50–150+ per property per month, none of them are AI-native, and none offer Sri Lankan-market commission-based economics with local support. YoHoBed 2.0's roadmap is built to win on exactly those axes:

Wave What it adds

AI running cost — a worked example
A typical Copilot question consumes roughly 10,000 input + 1,000 output tokens. On Claude Haiku 4.5 (US$1 in / $5 out per million tokens) that is ≈ $0.015 per question; on Claude Sonnet 5 ($3/$15) for complex analysis ≈ $0.045. An owner asking 100 questions a month costs ≈ $1.50–4.50/property/month — comfortably packaged inside a premium tier, and a feature competitors don't have at any price.
